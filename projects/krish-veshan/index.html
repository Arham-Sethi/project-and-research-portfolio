Field inquiry and farmer priorities

Krish Veshan is a 2023-24 agricultural social-research project. The supplied dissertation calls its organization KrishVeshan and its dissertation Seeds For Change. It combines farmer conversations, stakeholder interviews and secondary research to identify barriers to agricultural diversification.

Research design

The survey sections document Bijalpur stakeholders and farmers in Kanadia, followed by interviews with Anu Tech Mushrooms and a mushroom resource expert. Social-interaction pages describe village visits and an Ayurved college visit. This is qualitative problem exploration: responses are presented narratively rather than as a statistically sampled population survey.

What participants reported

Reported concerns include unreliable irrigation and electricity, expensive inputs, weak access to scheme information and uncertainty about new methods. Some farmers expressed openness to diversification only after tangible proof, funding and regular training. That conditional interest is an important design requirement; it does not establish that the proposed practices were adopted.

Diversification and technical options

The proposal connects farmer welfare to practical diversification options: mushroom cultivation, medicinal plants and soilless systems. The dissertation explains mechanisms and presents diagrams, but its proposed demonstration activities should remain distinguishable from measured crop trials.

Mushroom route

The proposal describes an indoor mushroom demonstration associated with Kanadia and advice from a former grower and resource expert. Its source diagrams explain substrate preparation, spawn and bag cultivation. Interviewees discuss low-cost kits and possible training camps. Such supplier statements are useful feasibility evidence but are not measured project profitability.

Other alternatives

Medicinal-plant cultivation is informed by an Ashtang Ayurved College interaction. Hydroponics is described as nutrient delivery without soil, while aeroponics suspends roots and applies nutrient mist. The document also considers abandoned mines for controlled-environment cultivation. These are explored alternatives; no production-scale deployment, resource-saving percentage or health benefit is established by their description.

Collective organization and training

The intervention logic is social as well as technical. Farmers may need access to training, capital, equipment and buyers before changing production. KrishVeshan proposes regular Kisan Sabhas and self-help groups as the coordination layer linking those needs.

Proposed group structure

The source proposes groups of 10-15 members, initial contributions of Rs500-1000 and community oversight. Village groups would connect through district-level coordination. Funds could support collective purchasing, equipment, training or assistance. These are design parameters of the proposal, not verified membership, deposits or lending activity.

Meeting and support model

A quarterly Kisan Sabha is proposed for farmers from Kanadia, Bijalpur, Jhalaria and Hingonia in collaboration with local authorities. Its agenda includes agricultural economics, new production techniques, scheme awareness, shared machinery and financial access. Keeping farmers’ own concerns central would make this a feedback process rather than only a lecture programme.

Outputs, learning and evidence limits

The supplied artifact documents research, field interactions, technical comparisons and an organizational proposal. These are substantive outputs even when later implementation and individual contribution records are incomplete. A clear account separates what was learned, what was designed and what was delivered.

Documented output

The strongest supported narrative is the connection between farmer-reported barriers and a combined diversification/training/collective-support proposal. Photographs accompany social-interaction accounts, while technical process diagrams explain the considered practices. Market charts and expert estimates support background reasoning; they should not be relabeled as results of a student-run agricultural trial.

What would establish impact

A follow-up programme record could identify training dates, participating farmers, actual demonstration inputs, harvests, expenses and repeat adoption. Comparisons should retain seasonal and market conditions. Reported profits from an interview or growth forecasts from secondary sources cannot substitute for project-level outcomes. Individual responsibility should be stated from contribution evidence rather than inferred from the document’s presence in a portfolio.
