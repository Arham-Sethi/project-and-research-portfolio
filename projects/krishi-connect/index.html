Community research and design brief

Krishi Connect is the title on the 2024-25 portfolio cover, while its approval sheet and conclusion use Droplets of Hope. The project links rural water access, farmer livelihoods and tribal employment, drawing on village questionnaires, interviews and an explanatory model.

Research process

Questionnaires concern Tillore Khurd and Chikli, Ghosikheda and Pathan Piplya. The document also records a telephone interview with Dr Kamlesh Kumar of Samdarshi Seva Kendra and a farming/irrigation discussion with Dr Sumer Singh. Visits identify seasonal shortages, limited technology access and difficulties navigating schemes. The narrative supplies local context, not a random-sample estimate.

From concern to requirement

Farmers describe dry wells or tanks, unaffordable alternatives and unreliable utilities. Some report local winter fog and existing bamboo cultivation. These observations motivate a locally supplied water-collection concept, but recollections of fog do not quantify recoverable water. The design must separately establish climate suitability, production capability and social acceptance.

Bamboo fog collection and water flow

The concept uses bamboo-fibre collection surfaces, drainage and storage to connect a fog-exposed location with downstream agricultural use. Its source illustrations show the proposed mechanism and landscape arrangement. They should be read as design explanations, with performance requiring site-specific measurement.

Mechanism and model

The portfolio describes hydrophilic and hydrophobic bamboo-fibre regions directing collected moisture toward tanks, with pipes transporting water downhill. Its model uses a 46.5 by30 inch inclined board to depict hilly terrain. Those dimensions describe the explanatory model, not the scale of an installed collector or a tested relationship to real hillside altitude.

Checking the yield calculation

Page32 assumes 8 litres per square metre per day and multiplies by56.6 square metres to obtain452.8 litres per day under favourable conditions. The same page gives net dimensions4 by3 metres, whose projected area is12 square metres; it does not transparently derive56.6. Effective fibre surface and collector frontal area cannot be interchanged without a defined model. Treat this as an unreconciled estimate, not a measured yield.

Bamboo supply and community delivery

The design links agricultural raw material, local fabrication and water-system maintenance. This social supply chain is central: technical equipment alone does not establish an affordable or maintained service. The portfolio proposes connecting growers, tribal producers, NGOs, existing self-help groups and panchayats.

Conditional willingness

Page34 reports interactions with20 farmers, of whom17 agreed to grow bamboo if offered market price under predetermined contract terms. This is conditional stated willingness in a small interviewed group. It is not evidence of planted acreage, signed contracts, delivered bamboo or sustained adoption, and should not be generalized as a population acceptance rate.

Proposed delivery structure

The source envisages NGO-mediated employment for tribal craftspeople, provision of drip kits to existing SHGs and panchayat oversight of distribution. It names bamboo poles/fibres and hemp or jute/twine as inputs. The future-tense implementation section indicates intended partnerships and roles; it does not establish that the named organizations committed resources or that the programme launched.

Circular resource use and outreach

Alongside water collection and drip delivery, the portfolio explores black soldier fly larvae for organic-waste processing and livelihood support. The work also documents public awareness activity. These strands share a resource-management theme while requiring different evidence to assess their effects.

BSFL as a researched option

The source describes larvae consuming organic material and producing possible feed and fertilizer-related outputs. It gives environmental ranges and broad resource-saving claims drawn from background research. No controlled rearing trial, waste-conversion measurement or verified water-saving outcome is documented here. Those quantities should therefore stay out of measured project results.

Documented communication

Page29 records a street play at Jhalaria village and Chhappan Dukaan to raise awareness of tribal welfare and water scarcity, accompanied by photographs. This supports an outreach activity claim. Attendance, changes in awareness and subsequent behaviour are not quantified, so impact should not be inferred from the presence of an event or photographs alone.

Validation priorities and project learning

Krishi Connect demonstrates an integrated research-and-design approach: listen to community constraints, study potential materials and mechanisms, and propose a governance structure for delivery. Its strongest current outputs are the questionnaires, recorded interactions, model and solution architecture.

Technical validation

A pilot should define frontal area, mesh material, porosity and orientation, then log collected volume with fog, wind and rainfall conditions. Rainfall contributions and storage losses need separate accounting. Any planned drinking-water use also needs appropriate water-quality assessment. These are validation requirements derived from the proposed uses, not completed tests.

Social and operational validation

A delivery trial should distinguish expressions of interest from actual contracts, employment and functioning systems. Recording training, payments, repair frequency, distribution rules and user feedback would test the proposed organization. The source’s title and model can be preserved while clearly presenting projected performance as a hypothesis and community-research findings as the documented foundation.
